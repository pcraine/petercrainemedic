Clinical Background

I am a practising Emergency Ambulance Crew (EAC) member with St John Ambulance North West, FREC 3 certified and currently progressing FREC 4 / Advanced Ambulance Practitioner. My clinical scope covers the full EAC column 9 of 10 — 19 medications including adrenaline IM, naloxone IM, salbutamol and ipratropium nebulisers, entonox, penthrox, GTN and aspirin — alongside comprehensive airway, trauma, assessment and monitoring capability. I am available for event medical cover across the North West.

I hold a full clinical deployment record: 32+ confirmed operational duties across sporting events, road races, large-scale public events, multi-agency exercises and community first aid. Clinical highlights include a nuclear site emergency planning exercise at Heysham NPP (5 simulated casualties, JESIP command huddles, Army EOD, fire, police and armed response); 25-patient Grand National deployment as zone lead clinician; suspected MI with 12-lead ECG acquisition and pre-alert to A&E; working at height trauma KED application at 25m with fire service extrication.

I maintain a full clinical log available on request.

"Every deployment runs to the same clinical standard — a sporting event crowd of fifty or a regional call with thousands present. The incident does not know the difference."

For Event Organisers
Does your event need EAC-level medical cover?
Expected attendance over 500 people
Physical activity — running, cycling, sport, combat or physical exertion
Alcohol or substance exposure is likely
Remote location or extended NWAS response time
Council, licensing authority or insurer requires a documented medical plan
If any of the above apply, a standard first aider is not sufficient. EAC-level cover closes the clinical gap between a first aid box and a 999 call — with medications, equipment and trained decision-making on site.
FREC 3 EAC · 19 Medications · Full Airway, Trauma & Assessment Capability
Clinical Assessment
Full primary & secondary survey, cABCDE adult & paediatric, vital signs monitoring, NEWS2 early warning score, FAST stroke assessment, AMTS, GCS, pupil assessment, clinical decision-making to EAC standard
Airway & Breathing
OPA, NPA, iGel supraglottic airway, BVM ventilation (single & two-person), mechanical & manual suction, oxygen therapy, capnography, airway emergency management including laryngectomy/tracheostomy patients
Medications
19 medications within EAC scope — adrenaline IM, naloxone IM, salbutamol NEB, ipratropium NEB, entonox INH, penthrox INH, hydrocortisone IM, glucagon IM, GTN spray SL, aspirin PO, paracetamol PO, ibuprofen PO, glucose gel PO, loratadine PO, ORS PO, oxygen INH, plus three patient-own medications
Trauma & Haemorrhage
Haemorrhage control, wound packing, tourniquet application (CAT x2 in kit), chest seal, haemostatic gauze, spinal immobilisation (KED, scoop stretcher, long board, spider harness), extrication techniques, major trauma management
Monitoring
12-lead ECG acquisition and interpretation, pre-alert to PPCI/stroke/A&E pathways, pulse oximetry, NIBP, glucometry, capnography, temperature monitoring
Documentation
Patient Report Forms (PRFs), cardiac arrest documentation, mass casualty triage documentation, ATMIST/SBAR/ASHICE handover, clinical records management to SJA and CQC standards
Assessing your patients
Full head-to-toe clinical assessment of any patient at your event — from a twisted ankle to a cardiac arrest. Proper clinical decision-making at point of contact, including when to escalate to 999 and what to tell them.
Keeping airways open
Equipment and skills to keep an unconscious patient breathing while an ambulance responds — including oxygen, suction and airway devices a standard first aider cannot use or carry.
19 medications on site
Adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Naloxone for opioid emergencies. Pain relief that works. Asthma treatment. Cardiac medications. Not “call 999 and wait.” Treatment starts at your event.
Managing trauma
Serious bleeding control, tourniquet application, wound packing and spinal management — everything needed to stabilise a seriously injured person and hand them over safely to NWAS.
Heart monitoring
A 12-lead ECG machine — the same device used in A&E — at your event. Peter can acquire and interpret it. If someone is having a heart attack, the hospital is pre-alerted before the ambulance leaves your site.
Documentation you can use
Every patient contact documented. Every medication recorded. Every NWAS handover formally logged. Written post-event medical report within 48 hours — suitable for insurance, licensing and council compliance.

Road Races & Running Events
Vehicle EAC · Dismounted · Treatment centre
Running events produce a predictable range of presentations: exertional illness, musculoskeletal injury, hypoglycaemia, exercise-induced asthma and cardiac events. What to ask any event medical provider
▼
Qualifications & Governance
✓
What is their clinical qualification level? First aid, FREC 3 or paramedic? FREC 3 EAC carries and administers 19 medications; a standard first aider carries none.
✓
Do they hold an Enhanced DBS certificate? Essential for any event with minors or vulnerable adults.
✓
Are they operating under a clinical governance framework? Who is clinically responsible for their decisions?
On The Day
✓
What medications will they carry? An EAC carries 19 including adrenaline, naloxone, pain relief and cardiac medications.
✓
Can they acquire a 12-lead ECG? Cardiac events are the leading cause of event fatality. Early ECG and pre-alert changes outcomes.
✓
What is their NWAS escalation protocol? How do they hand over to an ambulance crew, and is it formally documented?
Documentation
✓
Do they provide a post-event medical report? You may need this for insurance, licensing or council requirements. Many providers don’t include it.
✓
Do they complete Patient Report Forms for every contact? Clinical documentation is a governance requirement, not optional.

Credentials & Clinical Scope

Qualifications

Peter holds a clinical credential stack that goes significantly beyond standard event first aid provision — qualified to FREC 3 EAC level, CTLLS L4 teaching authority, full blue-light driving and a 19-medication scope. Every qualification listed here is current, evidenced, and maintained under St John Ambulance clinical governance.

That means structured patient assessment, a 19-medication formulary, 12-lead ECG capability, and the authority to pre-alert receiving hospitals. The credentials below are the evidence. Peter Craine is a Blackburn man. Born there, raised there, still there — not by inertia, but by intention. In a world that tends to measure ambition by how far a person has travelled from where they started, Peter has quietly measured his by how deeply he has invested in the place and the people he comes from. That single fact — stubbornly, proudly Lancashire — is the bedrock beneath everything else.

His move into pre-hospital care was never a sudden decision. It was older than that — a pull that surfaced again and again across every chapter of his working life. Wherever Peter was, whatever the organisation, he was the one who stepped forward when someone needed help. Not because protocol demanded it. Because he was constitutionally incapable of doing otherwise. That instinct — humanity in its most practical form — has been the constant thread through everything he has done.

For eighteen years before joining St John Ambulance, he worked as an IT manager. That career ended when his employer closed during the Covid pandemic. Where many would have experienced that as collapse, Peter experienced it as clarity. He turned toward the work he had always been drawn to, and he brought everything with him: the systems thinking, the operational calm, the ability to hold complexity without losing sight of the person at the centre of it. A varied career does not dilute a clinician. It sharpens one.

"If I can stabilise a patient at home and ensure they are safe, that is a far better outcome than a blue-light run. Not transporting is sometimes the very best thing I can do."

As an Emergency Ambulance Crew member certified to FREC 3, Peter is fully trained by St John Ambulance to operate an emergency ambulance — including the use of blue lights and sirens — and responds with a clinical capability that covers the overwhelming majority of emergencies people will ever face. He brings to every scene the quiet authority of someone committed to excellence — pride not in the role, but in what the role allows him to do for people. He will tell you plainly that if he is at your side, you are in the best possible hands.

What motivates him is not the adrenaline that outsiders tend to project onto this work. It is the outcome. A good shift is measured in whether the people he attended were left safer than when he arrived. He carries the profession's most honest burden — that you are with a patient for only a fraction of their journey — with the kind of accountability that never looks for an easy exit. He shows up regardless. That is the whole point.

The Leader

For the past twelve months, Peter has served as Community Network Lead for East Lancashire — simultaneously covering seven vacant sub-lead positions across event delivery, operational support, training, volunteering services, community engagement, youth services, and the network presidency. Responsiveness is easy to print on a wall. Peter has demonstrated it by simply never walking past a gap that needed filling.

The reward is watching a new volunteer step onto their first shift — that first surge of purpose — and getting to feel it again through their eyes. Teamwork at its best is not only what a crew achieves at scene. It is what a leader builds over time: a community of people who are capable, confident, and genuinely cared for.

St John Ambulance drew him because it carries everything that is good about care without the corporate shadow. He found its values and made them operational — not as a poster, but as a way of moving through the world. His ambition — stated plainly, without apology — is to become a paramedic. This is not a detour. It is the direction.

The Person

Away from the ambulance and the training room, Peter is — at his absolute core — a family man. Five grown children. Seven grandchildren. One great-grandchild. A life built wide and deep in the community he has never left. Outside of that: woodland walks, cold water swimming, cycling, and a genuine curiosity about artificial intelligence that keeps his IT roots alive and useful.

Then there are the details that stop people mid-sentence: he has represented England in amateur football and rugby; he has worked as a scare actor; he stood guard of honour at the Cenotaph — his proudest moment in uniform; and he has met Larry the Cat at 10 Downing Street. He resists easy categorisation, and does so entirely on purpose.

The job has handed Peter one lesson that no qualification teaches: life changes in the blink of an eye. He has seen it on scene more times than most people will encounter in a lifetime. That knowledge does not make him anxious. It makes him present — stripping away everything that does not matter and leaving only what does.

"I care. I cannot stand by and see someone hurt or in distress and not help."

That is not a mission statement. It is simply the truth of who he is — the same truth that made him step forward in every room he was ever in, long before he had a uniform to step forward in.

Everything else is detail.
